Musty smell, dark spots and bubbling paint: signs of mold after water damage

Mold announces itself in a few ways. A musty or earthy smell that returns after cleaning, dark or fuzzy patches on walls and ceilings, paint that bubbles or peels and spots on the back of a closet wall are the usual signs. They often show up weeks after a leak that nobody noticed.

  • A musty smell in a bathroom, basement, closet or attic.
  • Black, green, white or gray patches on drywall, wood or grout.
  • Peeling paint or wallpaper near a past leak.
  • Visible condensation on windows or cold walls.
  • Mold under a sink, behind a toilet or along a window sill.

If you see these signs after water damage, call. If the water is still active, the line is open 24/7 for emergencies, and the water comes first.

What mold needs to grow, and why water damage sets it off

Mold needs moisture, a food source and time. Drywall paper, wood, insulation and carpet are all food. Moisture is the part that can be controlled. The EPA says mold cannot grow without it, and advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours.

That is why the best mold control is fast water removal and drying. See structural drying for how drying is measured.

Can you clean mold yourself? What the EPA says about small areas

The EPA says mold covering about 10 square feet or less, roughly a 3 foot by 3 foot patch, can usually be handled by the homeowner on non-porous surfaces. Wear gloves, eye protection and a mask rated for the job. Do not mix bleach with other cleaners. Fix the water source first, or the mold returns.

Porous materials such as ceiling tile, carpet and soaked drywall are normally removed, not scrubbed. Mold can grow into them, and surface cleaning does not reach it.

When to call a mold remediation company

  • The affected area is larger than about 10 square feet.
  • Mold returns after you clean it.
  • The growth is inside walls, under flooring or in an HVAC system.
  • Contaminated floodwater or sewage caused the moisture.
  • You are preparing a home for sale or a lender requires clearance.

How mold remediation works: containment, removal and drying

  1. Find and stop the moisture. Remediation fails if water is still reaching the area. A plumber or roofer fixes the source.
  2. Contain the work area. Plastic barriers, negative air machines and HEPA air filtration keep spores from spreading to clean rooms.
  3. Remove contaminated materials. Moldy drywall, insulation, carpet and other porous items are bagged and removed. Solid surfaces are cleaned with HEPA vacuums and damp wiping.
  4. Dry the structure. Dehumidifiers and air movers dry remaining materials until readings are normal.
  5. Verify and rebuild. The area is checked, and repairs begin after it passes.

Practices in the industry follow the IICRC S520 standard for professional mold remediation. Naming the standard does not mean every job is identical. Each plan depends on the size of the area and the materials.

Mold testing, inspection and clearance: what each means

Inspection is a visual and moisture check by a qualified assessor to find where growth is and why it is there. Testing may include air or surface samples that a laboratory analyzes. Clearance, or post-remediation verification, is a check after remediation to confirm the area is clean and dry.

For clearance, many people hire an independent assessor who did not do the remediation work. That keeps the person judging the result separate from the person doing the work. Lenders, buyers and insurers sometimes ask for this paperwork.

Mold remediation, mold removal and mold cleaning compared

People use these terms loosely. Cleaning handles small surface growth on hard surfaces. Removal takes out materials that mold has grown into. Remediation is the full process, which includes containment, removal, drying and verification, and the correction of the moisture problem.

Where mold shows up in New Brunswick homes

Bathrooms

Showers, grout, caulk and exhaust fan ceilings stay damp. See shower leaks and tile failures.

Basements and crawl spaces

Seepage, high humidity and poor airflow keep surfaces damp.

Attics and top floors

Roof leaks and poor ventilation leave insulation and decking wet.

Closets and exterior walls

Cold corners collect condensation and hold stored items against damp walls.

What affects the cost of mold remediation

Cost depends on the size of the affected area, how many materials need removal, how hard the area is to reach, how much containment is required and whether testing and clearance are included. We explain the scope and price before work starts. Moisture repairs by a plumber or roofer are separate.

Mold and your insurance policy

The New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance advises policyholders to check their policy for mold coverage. Some policies cover mold that results from a covered water loss, and many limit or exclude it. Read your policy, ask your insurer and keep records of the water source.

Mold, home sales and loans

Buyers, lenders and inspectors may ask about past water damage and mold. Keep invoices, moisture readings, photos and any clearance report. If mold is found during a sale, remediation and a clearance check are common steps. Ask your agent or attorney what disclosure rules apply to you.

Mold and damp conditions in New Brunswick buildings

The Livingston Avenue Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1996, is one example of New Brunswick's older building stock. Older masonry and wood buildings often have limited insulation and ventilation, which can leave cold surfaces and corners damp.

After the Raritan overtopped its banks during Hurricane Ida in 2021, wet basements and first floors were at risk from slow drying. Drying quickly matters more than any product used afterward.
