How to Read a Water Damage Estimate in New Brunswick, NJ

A good estimate shows what will be done, where and why. Here is how to read one.

By the New Brunswick Water Damage Pros team. Published November 13, 2026.

Water damage estimates look confusing because they mix emergency services, equipment and rebuild work. A clear estimate breaks them apart so you can see what you are paying for and what your insurer is likely to review. This guide walks through the sections and the red flags.

The three parts of most water damage estimates

Part.What it covers.Examples.
Mitigation.Stopping further damage.Extraction, removal of wet materials, drying equipment, monitoring.
Cleaning and sanitizing.Making surfaces safe.Antimicrobial treatment, contents cleaning, odor control.
Reconstruction.Rebuilding what was removed.Drywall, insulation, flooring, paint, trim.

Some companies do all three. Others only mitigate and hand reconstruction to a builder. Ask which parts are included.

Reading the line items

Each line should describe a task, a room or area and a unit such as square feet or days. A line that says 'water damage cleanup' with one lump sum gives you little to compare. Look for room-by-room detail.

  • Extraction: area or time, and the type of equipment.
  • Demolition: which materials, how many square feet.
  • Equipment: number of air movers and dehumidifiers and the number of days.
  • Antimicrobial or cleaning treatment: area covered.
  • Monitoring: how often moisture readings will be taken.

Why equipment days matter

Drying equipment is usually billed per unit per day. The estimate is a forecast, and the final number depends on how fast the structure dries. A good company tracks moisture readings and removes equipment when the structure reaches dry goals, instead of keeping it running by default.

What a scope note should say

A scope note describes what is wet, what will be removed, what will be dried in place and what is outside the estimate. It should mention assumptions, such as 'carpet pad removed in the living room' or 'subfloor dry is assumed'. If conditions change, the company should explain the change before doing extra work.

Red flags in a water damage estimate

  • A single lump sum with no breakdown.
  • Pressure to sign before you have seen the scope.
  • A request for a large deposit before work begins.
  • No mention of moisture readings or documentation.
  • Vague wording such as 'as needed' with no limit.

A sample set of estimate line items, explained

Line item.Unit.What it tells you.
Water extraction, living room.Square feet.Area of floor that was wet.
Remove and dispose of carpet pad.Square feet.How much pad was taken out.
Air mover, per day.Each per day.Number of fans and days of use.
Dehumidifier, per day.Each per day.Equipment size and run time.
Cut drywall 2 feet above the floor.Linear feet.Length of wall opened.
Moisture monitoring visit.Each visit.Frequency of readings.
Antimicrobial treatment.Square feet.Area treated.

These are examples of how items are described, not prices. Prices vary by company and by job. What matters is that each line has a unit and a location.

Questions to ask before you sign

  • What is included in the total, and what is excluded?
  • How will you tell me when extra work is needed?
  • How many days of drying equipment do you expect?
  • Who handles the insurance paperwork and photos?
  • What is the payment schedule?

Why your insurer's number may differ

Insurers use pricing guides and may adjust items they consider too high or unnecessary. A gap between the estimate and the insurer's figure does not always mean one is wrong. Ask for the reasons, and provide moisture logs and photos that support the scope.

Common estimate terms in plain words

Term.Meaning.
Mitigation.Work that limits further damage, such as extraction and drying.
Demolition or tear-out.Removing materials that cannot be saved.
Containment.Plastic barriers that isolate a work area.
Pack-out.Moving belongings out of the affected rooms.
Reconstruction.Rebuilding walls, floors and finishes.
Overhead and profit.A markup that some contractors add on larger jobs.

Key takeaways

  • A good estimate lists tasks, rooms and units.
  • Equipment days are forecasts that depend on drying progress.
  • A written scope protects you and the contractor.
  • Compare scopes, not just totals.

How to compare two estimates fairly

  1. Put them side by side. List each room and each task in both estimates.
  2. Match the scope. Check whether both include extraction, demolition, drying and monitoring.
  3. Compare equipment. Look at the number of air movers and dehumidifiers and the days assumed.
  4. Check exclusions. One estimate may leave out cleaning, disposal or rebuild work.
  5. Ask about differences. Ask each company why its scope differs and what happens if conditions change.

The lowest total is not always the best deal if it leaves work out. The highest is not always the most thorough either. A clear scope and a company willing to explain each line are better signs than the number at the bottom.

A scenario: two estimates for the same wet living room

Two companies look at a living room where a pipe leaked. One estimate lists extraction, removal of pad, four air movers for three days, a dehumidifier and two monitoring visits. The other lists a single line for water cleanup and a larger total.

The owner asks the second company to break out the line. The company explains that it expects more demolition after opening the wall, and it agrees to put the assumptions in writing. The owner now has two comparable scopes and can ask questions about the difference.

Hiring rules that apply to New Jersey contractors

New Jersey's Contractors' Registration Act requires home improvement contractors to register with the Division of Consumer Affairs. Registration numbers begin with 13VH, and a written contract is required for work above $500. Registration is not an endorsement of quality.

The state treats a request for more than a third of the payment before work begins as a warning sign. Ask for the registration number and write it on your copy of the estimate.
