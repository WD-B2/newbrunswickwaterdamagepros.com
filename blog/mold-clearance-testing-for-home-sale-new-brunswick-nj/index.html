Mold Clearance Testing for a Home Sale in New Brunswick, NJ

Buyers and lenders sometimes ask for proof that mold was handled. Clearance is how that proof is made.

By the New Brunswick Water Damage Pros team. Published November 13, 2026.

If mold shows up during a sale, refinance or inspection, the conversation soon turns to clearance. Clearance is a check after remediation to confirm the area is clean and dry. This guide explains what it is, who performs it and what paperwork to keep.

What clearance testing means

Clearance, also called post-remediation verification, usually combines a visual inspection, moisture readings and sometimes air or surface sampling. The aim is to confirm that visible growth is gone, materials are dry and the work area is ready for rebuild.

It does not certify the whole building, and it does not predict the future. It documents the condition of the remediated area on the day.

Who should do it

Many homeowners use an independent assessor who did not perform the remediation. That separates the person judging the work from the person who did it. Ask whether your state licenses mold assessors and remediators, and check any requirement before hiring.

What a clearance report may include

Item.Purpose.
Scope of the area checked.Defines what was cleared.
Visual inspection notes and photos.Shows the condition after remediation.
Moisture readings.Confirms materials are dry.
Sampling results, if collected.Compares air or surfaces with a reference.
Assessor's signature and date.Documents who did the check and when.

Timing during a sale or loan

  1. Find out what the buyer or lender wants. Some ask for a specific report or an assessor's credentials.
  2. Fix the moisture source. Clearance means little if water is still coming in.
  3. Complete remediation. Follow a written scope.
  4. Schedule the clearance check. Allow time before closing.
  5. Share the paperwork. Provide the report, the invoices and photos.

Disclosure and legal questions

Rules about what sellers must disclose vary. Ask your real estate agent or attorney what applies to your sale. This guide is general information and not legal advice.

What to ask an assessor before you hire one

  • Do you have training or credentials in mold assessment, and does my state require licensing?
  • Will you be independent of the remediation company?
  • What does your report include?
  • What sampling, if any, will you collect, and why?
  • How long until I receive the report?

Ask for a written price and a sample report. A clear answer to these questions is a good sign.

What clearance does not cover

A clearance report covers the area that was checked. It does not examine every room in the house, and it does not predict future moisture. If a new leak occurs, the report no longer describes the home.

Buyers sometimes expect clearance to function like a warranty. It is documentation, not a guarantee.

A sample timeline for a sale

Stage.What happens.
Inspection finds moisture or growth.The buyer asks for investigation or repair.
Source repaired.A plumber, roofer or drainage contractor fixes the cause.
Remediation.Growth and affected materials are removed and the area is dried.
Clearance check.An assessor verifies the area.
Paperwork shared.Reports and invoices go to the buyer and lender.

Myths about clearance

One myth is that clearance is a legal requirement in every sale. Requirements vary, and buyers or lenders often set the expectation. Another is that clearance guarantees a mold-free home. It documents the condition of the checked area on that day.

A third myth is that any clean air sample proves the home is fine. Sampling is one tool among several, and results depend on how and where samples are taken.

Key takeaways

  • Clearance is a check after remediation, often by an independent assessor.
  • Fix the moisture source before testing.
  • Keep reports, invoices and drying logs together.
  • Ask your agent or attorney about disclosure and lender requirements.

How buyers and lenders usually respond to mold findings

Buyers react in different ways. Some ask the seller to remediate before closing, some ask for a price reduction and some walk away. Lenders may require that visible mold and moisture problems be corrected before they approve funds.

Sellers who have records ready are in a stronger position. A folder with the leak repair invoice, the moisture readings, the remediation scope and the clearance report answers the questions buyers ask first.

If a buyer's inspector finds mold, ask for the inspector's report and photographs, then get your own assessment before you negotiate. The two views may differ, and a second opinion from an independent assessor can narrow the disagreement.

A scenario: mold found during a buyer's inspection

A buyer's inspector finds dark growth on basement drywall and a high moisture reading near the wall. The buyer asks for remediation before closing. The seller hires a plumber to fix a small leak, hires a remediation company to remove the drywall and dry the wall, and brings in an independent assessor for clearance.

The seller hands the buyer the plumber's invoice, the drying log, the remediation scope and the assessor's report. The buyer's lender reviews the packet. The sale continues because each question has a document behind it.

Selling or buying with water history in New Brunswick

New Brunswick is the county seat of Middlesex County, and its housing market includes older homes, rentals and multi-unit buildings. Buyers in flood-prone areas, including parts of the Raritan corridor affected by Hurricane Ida in 2021, often ask about past water and mold.

Keep records of repairs, drying logs and clearance reports in one folder. Flood insurance status is also a common question for buyers, and the New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance notes that standard homeowners policies exclude flood.
